About

I study how a warming climate reshapes financial risk for the households least equipped to absorb it. My work sits at the intersection of household finance and climate finance, focused on physical climate risk — the kind that shows up in mortgage terms, insurance non-renewals, and retirement-account performance long before it shows up in a headline.

I'm particularly interested in closing a measurement gap: the empirical literature on physical climate risk still lacks a toolkit as rigorous as the one built for transition risk. I want to combine geospatially linked household financial data — HMDA mortgage records, FEMA and NOAA hazard exposure, and state insurance-filing data — with quasi-experimental methods to test which measures of physical risk actually predict household financial distress.

I'm currently an MSc Quantitative Finance candidate at the Spears School of Business, Oklahoma State University, where coursework in econometrics, stochastic calculus, and computational finance is building the toolkit this agenda demands. I hold a BSc in Business Administration, with a minor in Finance and Economics, from Carnegie Mellon University.

Research Interests

I aim to help build the measurement toolkit that household and climate finance still lacks — connecting physical climate risk to the balance sheets of the households who actually carry it.

Physical Climate Risk & Household Balance Sheets

The literature on physical climate risk trails well behind the transition-risk literature: engineering hazard scores are granular but often proprietary, geospatial measures struggle to capture true exposure, and market-based measures are contaminated by everything else that moves prices. I test which of these measures — engineering hazard scores, realized disaster losses, or house-price discounts — best predicts real household financial distress, such as delinquency, forced relocation, or a drop in retirement savings.

Causal Identification & Adaptation

Building on work showing that drought and heat exposure raise firms' cost of capital once exposure is measured with enough granularity to matter, I plan to use quasi-exogenous timing — state-level insurer exits or FEMA disaster declarations — as an instrument for local physical-risk repricing. I'm also interested in how adaptation capacity — insurance coverage, savings buffers, geographic mobility — explains why two similarly exposed households can end up with very different financial outcomes after the same event.
