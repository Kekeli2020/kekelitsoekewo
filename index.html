About

I study how a warming climate reshapes financial risk for the households least equipped to absorb it. My work sits at the intersection of household finance and climate finance, focused on physical climate risk, the kind that shows up in mortgage terms, insurance non-renewals, and retirement account performance long before it shows up in a headline.

I am particularly interested in closing a measurement gap. The empirical literature on physical climate risk still lacks a toolkit as rigorous as the one built for transition risk. I want to combine geospatially linked household financial data, including HMDA mortgage records, FEMA and NOAA hazard exposure, and state insurance filing data, with quasi-experimental methods to test which measures of physical risk actually predict household financial distress.

Alongside this research agenda, I work hands-on with large, real-world datasets through applied projects, including an NSF I-Corps venture automating insurance claims workflows for personal injury firms, a machine learning model that flags fraudulent bank transactions, and a duration-hedging strategy built for a $325 million fixed-income portfolio. This project work, together with my graduate coursework in econometrics, stochastic calculus, and computational finance, is building the technical toolkit this research agenda demands.

I am currently an MSc Quantitative Finance candidate at the Spears School of Business, Oklahoma State University. I hold a BSc in Business Administration, with a minor in Finance and Economics, from Carnegie Mellon University.

Research Interests

I aim to help build the measurement toolkit that household and climate finance still lacks, connecting physical climate risk to the balance sheets of the households who actually carry it.

Physical Climate Risk & Household Balance Sheets

The literature on physical climate risk trails well behind the transition-risk literature: engineering hazard scores are granular but often proprietary, geospatial measures struggle to capture true exposure, and market-based measures are contaminated by everything else that moves prices. I test which of these measures, engineering hazard scores, realized disaster losses, or house-price discounts, best predicts real household financial distress, such as mortgage delinquency or insurance non-renewals.

Causal Identification & Adaptation

I am interested in using quasi-experimental methods, such as observing state-level insurer exits or FEMA disaster declarations, to isolate local physical-risk repricing. The objective is to examine how adaptation mechanisms like insurance coverage, savings buffers, and geographic mobility dictate the financial outcomes of similarly exposed households following climate shocks.
